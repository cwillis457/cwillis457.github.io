Book Review

George Marshall

Defender of the Republic — by David L. Roll

Reviewed by COL (Ret) Chris R. Willis

AuthorDavid L. Roll
Published2019 · Dutton Caliber
Length704 pages
CategoryBiography · Leadership
Best ForLeaders who develop leaders
Author’s Prior WorkThe Hopkins Touch

Ask a room of Army officers to name the most consequential American soldier of the twentieth century and the debate will bounce between Patton, Eisenhower, and MacArthur. The correct answer never commanded troops in combat in either World War. George C. Marshall — Chief of Staff of the Army through the Second World War, Secretary of State, Secretary of Defense, and architect of the plan that rebuilt Europe — won the war from an office, and David L. Roll’s George Marshall: Defender of the Republic makes the case that his weapon was character. For anyone in the business of developing leaders, this biography is less a history than a field manual.

Roll is Senior Counsel at Steptoe & Johnson and the author of The Hopkins Touch, his well-regarded biography of Franklin Roosevelt’s adviser Harry Hopkins. Drawing on papers and personal letters that have only become available in recent decades, Roll produces a portrait more intimate than earlier treatments — Thomas Ricks judged it possibly the best of the Marshall biographies, and it supplements rather than supersedes Forrest Pogue’s monumental four-volume standard. Roll wisely compresses the early years and moves quickly to where the lessons are: Marshall at work, building the Army that won the war.

The first lesson is talent management as a weapon system. When Marshall became Chief of Staff in 1939 — sworn in the day Germany invaded Poland — the U.S. Army ranked behind Portugal’s in size. Within four years he grew it more than fortyfold, and his scarcest resource was never equipment but leaders. Marshall famously kept a book on officers he encountered throughout his career, and when mobilization came he reached into it ruthlessly: elevating unproven talents like Eisenhower and Bradley over hundreds of more senior officers, and retiring or reassigning those who could not grow at the speed of the war. Every leader who has struggled to balance loyalty to individuals against duty to the mission will recognize the weight of those decisions. Marshall teaches that assessing and positioning talent is not an administrative task delegated to personnel officers — it is the commander’s decisive act.

The second lesson is candor as a career strategy — one that seems suicidal and proves indispensable. Roll returns repeatedly to Marshall’s willingness to deliver unwelcome truths directly to power, beginning with a 37-year-old Captain Marshall confronting General Pershing in France and continuing through his first meeting with President Roosevelt, where Marshall flatly disagreed with the President while more senior men nodded along. Roosevelt chose him as Chief of Staff over thirty-three more senior generals precisely because of it. Marshall then institutionalized the practice, demanding that his own staff bring him disagreement rather than concurrence. The modern term is psychological safety; Marshall built it decades before the vocabulary existed, and from the top down.

The third lesson is selflessness as strategic capital. The pivotal moment of the book is the command of Operation Overlord — the assignment Marshall wanted more than any other and the capstone every soldier of his generation dreamed of. Roosevelt left the choice effectively in Marshall’s hands, and Marshall refused to ask for it, telling the President to act in the national interest. Eisenhower got the command and the fame; Marshall stayed in Washington because Roosevelt could not sleep soundly with him out of the country. Roll’s argument — persuasive to this reviewer — is that this accumulated reputation for putting the country first is exactly what later allowed a general to persuade a Republican Congress to fund the Marshall Plan for a Democratic administration. Trust, built over decades of visibly subordinating self-interest, became the decisive instrument of American statecraft.

Roll does not write a hagiography. He addresses Marshall’s refusal to desegregate the Army during the war, the failed 1946 mission to unify China, and his fumbling over recognition of Israel — failures of moral imagination and of mission, honestly weighed. The book has structural weaknesses too: transitions between Marshall’s posts sometimes arrive abruptly, and readers hoping to understand how Marshall’s pre-military years forged his character will find that ground lightly covered. These are acceptable costs for a single-volume treatment that keeps its focus where it belongs.

In summary, David Roll’s George Marshall: Defender of the Republic belongs on the professional reading shelf of anyone who develops leaders or aspires to institutional transformation. Marshall took an army ranked behind Portugal’s and built the force that won a world war — not primarily through technology or doctrine, but through the deliberate assessment, development, and courageous placement of leaders. In an era when the Army is again transforming under pressure, Marshall’s example endures: institutions are transformed by character operating at scale.
