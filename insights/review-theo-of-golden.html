From the WSG Desk

Theo of Golden

A novel of Columbus, Georgia — by Allen Levi

A note from the desk of COL (Ret) Chris R. Willis

AuthorAllen Levi
Published2025 · Atria Books (self-pub 2023)
CategoryFiction · A Columbus Story
DistinctionNYT bestseller · 750,000+ copies
Setting“Golden” — a river town like Columbus
Best ForAnyone who calls this valley home

Every so often a book belongs to a place as much as to its author, and Columbus, Georgia now has one. Theo of Golden, the debut novel by Columbus native Allen Levi, began as a quietly self-published story in 2023 and has become a genuine phenomenon — picked up by Atria Books, an imprint of Simon & Schuster, in 2025, climbing to number one among all titles on Amazon in early 2026, and selling well past 750,000 copies. “It’s the book that sold itself,” one of the people who helped launch it has said. What earns it a note from this desk is not the sales figure. It is that the luminous, river-threaded town Levi calls Golden is unmistakably our own.

The premise is deceptively gentle. An elderly, watchful stranger named Theo arrives in Golden and stays for about a year. He haunts a coffee shop, learns the rhythms of the streets, and undertakes a quiet campaign of attention: with the help of an artist friend, Asher Glissen, he commissions portraits of ordinary townspeople and gives each one to its subject, along with a few words of encouragement. No one is quite sure who Theo is or why he has come. What unfolds is less a plot than an accumulation of grace — a study of what happens to a community when someone decides, deliberately, to see the people in it. “In every face,” Theo says, “I detect sadness,” and the novel’s quiet argument is that joy and sorrow are not opposites but neighbors — living on the same street, in the same face, at the same time.

Golden is a southern city on a river much like the Chattahoochee, and readers here will recognize the bones of home. The novel’s coffee shop has a real-world cousin in Fountain City Coffee, where the portraits of Columbus artist Garry Pound hang on the walls — the evident seed of Asher Glissen’s portrait-making. The resemblance is strong enough that Visit Columbus has built a self-guided tour of the real places behind the story, and the book now draws readers into the city the way a good novel always sends people looking for the streets it was dreamed on. That is a rare civic gift. A region spends years and fortunes trying to put itself on the map; Theo of Golden did it with a coffee shop, a river, and an old man’s habit of paying attention.

Allen Levi is Columbus through and through. He grew up here, took his law degree at the University of Georgia, practiced in town and made partner, and later served two years as a probate judge in neighboring Harris County before giving himself to writing and to a long career as a songwriter and recording artist. He lives now about thirty miles north, near Hamilton. He wrote the novel in his sixties, beginning around 2019 and working at it for some three years, and he drew Theo in part from the memory of his late brother, Gary, a missionary who died in 2012. The book reads like the work of a man who has practiced, for a long time and in several trades, the single discipline it celebrates: looking closely, and with affection, at the people in front of him.

Our work at this desk usually trains its eye on the hard architecture of this region — the installations, the industry, the modernization dollars, the workforce that staffs them. Theo of Golden is a reminder of what all of that is finally for. A town worth investing in is first a town worth seeing, and Levi has given Columbus a portrait of itself as a place where ordinary kindness is still possible and still consequential. That is not soft sentiment; it is civic capital. The same qualities that make a community generous on the page — attention, memory, a willingness to know your neighbors — are the ones that make it resilient off the page, in the harder work this firm tracks week to week. I recommend the book warmly, and not only to readers from here. But if you are from here, read it the way you would walk the RiverWalk at dusk: slowly, and grateful for the place.
