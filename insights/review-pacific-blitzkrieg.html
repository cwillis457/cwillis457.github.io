Book Review

Pacific Blitzkrieg

World War II in the Central Pacific — by Sharon Tosi Lacey

Reviewed by COL (Ret) Chris R. Willis

AuthorSharon Tosi Lacey
Published2013 · University of North Texas Press
Length282 pages · 35 photos · 5 maps
CategoryMilitary History · Joint Operations
Best ForLeaders integrating organizations under pressure
Author’s BackgroundWest Point graduate · US Army officer · PhD, Leeds

The word “joint” rolls easily off the modern military tongue — joint task forces, joint doctrine, joint professional military education. It was not always so. In Pacific Blitzkrieg: World War II in the Central Pacific, Sharon Tosi Lacey examines how the US Army and Marine Corps were forced to merge into a single striking force across five major invasions — Guadalcanal, Tarawa, the Marshalls, Saipan, and Okinawa — while a global war raged around them. Integrating two proud services with different doctrines, equipment, and cultures would have been difficult in peacetime; doing it under fire, at corps and division level, was a monumental task. That task, and the strained but ultimately successful partnership it produced, is the real subject of this book.

Lacey is well credentialed to tell this story. A West Point graduate who served more than twenty-five years as a US Army officer, she earned her PhD in military history from the University of Leeds; Pacific Blitzkrieg is the revision of her doctoral dissertation, built on primary research across more than twenty archival repositories. That soldier-scholar combination shows on every page. Lacey writes with a practitioner’s eye for command relationships, task organization, and staff mechanics — the unglamorous machinery that historians without uniformed service often pass over. Notably, she also fills a genuine gap: despite the thousands of works on the Pacific War, no one before had examined the detailed mechanics of Army–Marine integration at the corps and division level.

The book’s throughline is the maturation of joint ground operations, and Lacey structures each campaign the same disciplined way: planning, preparation, execution, and the lessons carried — or not carried — into the next fight. At Guadalcanal, the services improvised. At Tarawa, the cost of amphibious inexperience was paid in blood in the lagoon. By Kwajalein and the Marshalls, hard lessons had hardened into repeatable technique. This is capability development on the fly: doctrine written between operations, equipment modified between landings, and raw recruits turned into efficient combat formations in months rather than years. Readers of this site will recognize the pattern — it is experimentation under consequence, with each invasion serving as both operation and learning event.

Lacey’s treatment of the famous “Smith versus Smith” controversy at Saipan — Marine Lieutenant General Holland “Howlin’ Mad” Smith’s relief of Army Major General Ralph Smith — is worth the price of the book alone. Using newly discovered primary sources, she moves past the tired service-partisan retellings and shows how one senior leader’s blatant partisanship and inability to work with others nearly stalled the American drive across the Pacific. The episode is a leadership case study with enduring relevance: interoperability is not primarily a technology problem or a doctrine problem. It is a leadership problem. Egos, service parochialism, and unclear command relationships can undo what liaison officers, common procedures, and shared graphics have painstakingly built.

If the book has a weakness, it is the title. As other reviewers have noted, a three-year, island-by-island grind is the opposite of a lightning war, and readers expecting a blitzkrieg narrative will instead find a methodical study of institutional learning. That is a marketing quibble, not a substantive one — and frankly, the methodical study is the more valuable book. A reader may also wish for deeper source annotation in places, a concession the publisher likely made for space.

The lessons travel well beyond 1945. Today’s Army and Marine Corps are again transforming under pressure — new formations, new unmanned systems, new theater postures aimed at the same Pacific geography. Multi-domain operations will demand integration not just between two ground services but across all domains, with joint and coalition partners, at echelons far below where “joint” traditionally lived. Lacey’s history reminds us that such integration is forged, not declared: it requires deliberate cross-service planning, honest after-action learning between operations, and above all, senior leaders who put the mission above the service patch on their shoulder.

In summary, Sharon Tosi Lacey’s Pacific Blitzkrieg is a fresh and necessary examination of how two services learned to fight as one under the most unforgiving conditions imaginable. Her soldier-scholar perspective, primary-source rigor, and focus on the neglected corps and division level make this a valuable addition to the Pacific War literature — and a case study in joint integration for the modern student of large-scale combat operations.
