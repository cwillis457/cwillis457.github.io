This Week’s Lead

A Faster Way to Sell Autonomy to the Army

The Army Applications Laboratory’s DevX Autonomy platform just put real money behind a simple idea: make it easier for the government to find and buy commercial autonomy. DevX Autonomy is a searchable catalog of pre-assessed vendor solutions — more than 90 capabilities so far — that lets a company submit once and be seen by multiple government buyers, cutting the time from discovery to contract. This month it drove more than $3.4 million in awards to two small businesses (Army.mil).

  • Hylio Inc. won more than $1.9 million for its ARES HYL-300, a scalable unmanned aircraft, through the Capability Program Executive for Mission Autonomy.
  • Marathon Targets Inc. won more than $1.5 million for an autonomous robotic training system supporting small-drone selection and training.

Why it matters

The mechanism is as important as the money. For a small firm, the hardest part of defense work is being found by the right buyer at the right time. A standing, pre-vetted catalog is one answer — and it is the first of several this week.

Mark Your Calendar — Opportunity

The Joint Innovation Outpost Opens Its Doors, Virtually

Here is a door you can walk through in the next five weeks. The U.S. Army’s Joint Innovation Outpost (JIOP) will host a virtual Industry Day on Tuesday, November 17, 2026, from 2:00 to 3:30 p.m. ET. Run under Army Materiel Command, JIOP exists to move commercial capability to operational units fast, working through three Operational Acquisition Detachments embedded with the Army’s corps: OAD 1 with XVIII Airborne Corps, OAD 2 with I Corps, and OAD 3 with III Corps. The session will cover JIOP’s mission, priorities, and upcoming events, walk through its “9-Line” submission overview, and take pre-submitted and live questions (SAM.gov, notice JIOP-2026-01).

The capability areas JIOP named read like a map of this newsletter’s recurring themes — and several are squarely in the Columbus/FT Benning region’s wheelhouse:

  • Autonomous and unmanned systems, “robotics on the move,” and robotics sustainment at the tactical edge
  • Counter-UAS and defensive fires; electronic warfare; signature management and deception
  • C2 architecture, decision dominance, and common-operating-picture development; AI, data, and automation
  • Power projection and power generation; casualty evacuation and tracking

Action

Registration is required, and the deadline to RSVP for the virtual session is Friday, November 6, 2026, at 6:00 p.m. ET. The RSVP also lets you submit a question in advance. If your company has capability in any of the areas above, this is a low-cost hour that tells you exactly how JIOP wants to be approached — register through the link in the SAM.gov notice.

The Bigger Picture

The Pentagon Retools Itself as a “Rapid Customer”

DevX and JIOP are the tactical edge of a strategic shift. This month the Office of the Under Secretary of War for Research and Engineering — through the new Assistant Secretary of War for Critical Technologies, Michael Dodd — partnered with the CIA and In-Q-Tel, the intelligence community’s nonprofit strategic investor, to scout and fast-track mature commercial technology into the force (Breaking Defense). In-Q-Tel does the scouting and technical due diligence; the Pentagon lines up the sponsors and transition pathways, with a benchmark of moving validated prototypes to field evaluation within twelve months.

Dodd’s own framing captures the change: the department is “moving from… a long-cycle technology developer to operating as an agile, credible, and rapid customer.” The initial focus areas — applied AI, biomanufacturing, contested logistics, quantum, scaled directed energy, and scaled hypersonics — sit mostly outside the ground-maneuver lane, and In-Q-Tel works through its own vetted network rather than an open call. So read this less as a door for the average small business and more as the clearest signal yet of where the whole system is heading: toward buying commercial, and buying it fast.

The People Behind the Robots

The Army Creates a Job for Making Robotics Work

Buying the technology is only half the problem; someone must make it work in a formation. The Army just graduated the first class of a brand-new warrant-officer specialty built for exactly that — the 390A Robotics Technician Integrator. Ten warrant officers completed the inaugural 19-week course, which blends artificial intelligence, software, mesh networking, and hands-on employment of unmanned ground vehicles, drones, sensors, and electronic-warfare systems (DefenseScoop).

The program’s architect, Chief Warrant Officer 5 Michael Updike, said the service identified “a gap in understanding at multiple echelons — from the brigade level and up — on how to integrate robotics into operations.” The first cohort came out of the special-operations community, but the Army has signaled interest in scaling the specialty service-wide. If that happens, it will eventually reach the maneuver force — and the question for our region is whether the training and talent pipeline that produces these integrators runs through the Maneuver Center of Excellence (MCoE). It is a thread worth watching.

Experimentation in the Field

Command and Control, Proven in the Dirt

For a picture of what all this looks like when it reaches Soldiers, look to Hawaii. The 25th Infantry Division spent late September running Nakoa Fleek, an exercise in its Lightning Surge campaign to rebuild command-and-control the hard way — in the field, not in a lab. Its 2nd Mobile Brigade worked advanced C2 software, sensors, end-user devices, small drones, Infantry Squad Vehicles, and new radios under realistic conditions, deliberately pushing past the point where a controlled test would stop (Army.mil).

One of the division’s officers put the philosophy well: “The controlled environment gets you to the 50-yard line. The last 50 yards happen out here.” That is the same conviction behind the Maneuver Battle Lab’s work and the CMI2 / Catalyst Pathfinder efforts we have covered since Issue 01 — that capability is proven by Soldiers in realistic conditions, not declared finished in a slide deck. The 25th is a Pacific unit, but the method is universal, and it is the method our region is built to run.

Valley of Death, reviewed

Every item above is about the government trying to buy commercial technology faster. Sharon Weinberger’s Valley of Death: How Big Tech Is Fueling the Future of War is the sharp, skeptical account of what that shift actually means — the rise of a “Pentagon–Silicon complex” in which venture-backed firms fund their own development, keep the intellectual property, and sell finished capability back to a government that is no longer the architect. Our review reads it through a capability-developer’s lens: the speed is real and necessary, but so is the question of who ends up owning the nation’s military advantage. Essential context for anyone selling into, or buying from, the new system.

01 For Business Owners & Small Business

  • Act now: register for the JIOP Virtual Industry Day by November 6 (event November 17) if you touch autonomy, counter-UAS, C2, EW, power, or robotics sustainment. It is the clearest look you will get at how one fast-moving Army buyer wants to be approached.

  • Still open: SBIR/STTR Release 6 closes October 21 (see Calendar).

02 For Investors

  • Autonomy is now a production-scale business, not a science project — DevX awards and JIOP’s priorities both point to sustained Army demand for unmanned systems and their enabling layers (power, comms, autonomy software).
  • The In-Q-Tel partnership is a strong signal of where DoD money and attention are heading: commercial-first, venture-speed. Watch which focus areas the department is willing to underwrite.
  • The talent question is real: the 390A specialty signals the Army knows fielding robotics requires people, not just hardware — a tailwind for firms whose products are usable by a unit, not just a lab.

03 For Government & Community Leaders

  • The common thread is acquisition speed — catalogs, outposts, consortia, and venture partnerships all aimed at closing the gap between a good idea and a contract. Helping regional firms navigate these on-ramps compounds the return.
  • Watch the 390A robotics specialty: if it scales service-wide, the training and talent pipeline is a natural fit for the Maneuver Center of Excellence.
