Columbus–Chattahoochee Valley Area Defense Dispatch

Issue 10 — Smarter, Not Smaller

A weekly briefing for owners, investors, government leaders, and small businesses in the Columbus–Fort Benning region.

Issue 10  ·  Week of November 23, 2026

Two documents landed this month that frame the Army’s autonomy moment better than any demonstration could. In the first, the Army’s recently retired Vice CSA argues that combined arms is being reinvented around agentic machines — that soon “the formation, not the platform, becomes the weapon.” In the second, published this week by the Royal United Services Institute (RUSI), the analyst whose book on future war we reviewed earlier this year delivers the sober footnote, and we close this issue with it: drones make an infantry battalion deadlier, but they do not make it smaller. Between those poles — the promise of autonomous mass and the stubborn arithmetic of the people it still takes to run it — sits the rest of this issue: an eighteen-drone swarm fired from a robot in Germany, a contracting machine that puts commercial technology in Soldiers’ hands in 128 hours, and the Army’s admission that, for now, one robotic mission in five still ends in an emergency stop.

This Week’s Lead

The Formation Becomes the Weapon

The most important argument in landpower this month did not come from a program office but from an essay. Writing for West Point’s Modern War Institute, retired Gen. James Mingus — until recently the 39th Vice Chief of Staff of the Army — makes the case that combined arms maneuver is being reinvented rather than discarded. The classic elements endure: forces still must sense, decide, mass, maneuver, strike, and sustain. What is changing “forever,” he writes, is how those effects are delivered — by agentic AI acting as the orchestrator and connective tissue across many human and autonomous systems. The value, in his telling, lies in the orchestration, not in any individual machine.

His formulation is disciplined about who is in charge. “Humans command, while AI and machines control,” Mingus writes; “humans own intent and purpose.” Mass does not disappear in a transparent battlespace — “it changes form,” achieved by dispersing forces and concentrating effects rather than bodies. The heterogeneous, self-reorganizing human-machine formation — reconnaissance, communications, jamming, deception, protection, and strike, woven together — becomes the real unit of combat power, which is why he argues “the formation, not the platform, becomes the weapon.” The through-line for anyone buying or building: measure combat power by the whole networked formation, command it at machine speed within human-set intent and risk, and procure the architecture that links everything rather than the best standalone box. The aim, he insists, “is not autonomous war, but human-directed war with autonomous means” — the human “must remain responsible for the purpose of violence even as machines increasingly execute its mechanics” (Modern War Institute).

Eighteen Drones From a Robot at Grafenwoehr

Two days after that essay was published, a live demonstration in Bavaria looked a great deal like Mingus’s formation on the ground. At the Grafenwoehr Training Area on November 12, a containerized uncrewed ground vehicle — the R3NEGADE — rolled out of a shipping container and launched an eighteen-drone first-person-view swarm from an onboard launcher. It did not act alone: a second ground robot carried twin anti-tank launchers, smoke-laying drones preceded the swarm, another carried a developmental anti-armor payload, and rocket-armed resupply drones ran in tandem. The event was staged by the DEVCOM Armaments Center (AC) with U.S. Army Europe and Africa and the 7th Army Training Command under an effort called the Remote Rapid Response Autonomous Contingency Team, and the Army framed it against its new “How the Army Fights” guidance — specifically the strike and endurance pillars — and the NATO Eastern Flank Deterrence Initiative (Army Recognition).

This was a technology demonstration, not a fielded capability: Army officials attached no procurement decision or timeline, some of the systems were remotely operated rather than fully autonomous, and the manufacturers were not named. The most useful number to come out of the Army’s broader experimentation is a humbling one — roughly one robotic mission in five still ends in an emergency stop. That figure, more than any swarm, is the true state of the art: the formation Mingus describes is arriving, but it is not yet dependable, and the gap between a compelling demonstration and a trustworthy capability is exactly the work ahead.

How Commercial Tech Reaches the Fight — Fast

If the Army wants the architecture that links everything, it must buy differently, and this fall it showed how. Through its Pathway for Innovation and Technology (PIT), the service is pairing three tools. Army FUZE supplies the money and the legal muscle — Technology Maturation Initiative (TMI) funding, experimental-purpose procurement authority under 10 U.S.C. § 4023, and a contracting center that can close agreements in hours rather than months. Operation Jailbreak, run under the Secretary of the Army’s “Right to Integrate” campaign, pushes vendors to pry open their proprietary interfaces and publish standard APIs to a new Army API Marketplace — because in a formation-is-the-weapon world, a system that cannot connect is a system the Army cannot use. And the Global Tactical Edge Acquisition Directorate (G-TEAD) turns that into fielded kit through its Needs-Driven Acquisition events: a compressed sprint in which only vendors that have “jailbroken” their systems may compete, Soldiers hold 51 percent of the selection vote, and awards land within days. At the August “Falcon Fury” event, a FUZE surge team signed four experimental-purpose agreements — for robots, sensors, autonomy software, and communications — within 128 hours of the first solicitation, with systems deployed days later (Army PIT).

The complement to that machine is prize money, and the Army FUZE xTech program is where to find it. Its Q4 snapshot reports a busy year — twelve new competitions, roughly $9.5 million in cash prizes, and some $107 million in follow-on small-business research contracts — with about two dozen competitions planned for the coming year. Several are open now and squarely on this issue’s themes: xTech|Disrupt Fires is putting up $20 million for low-cost cruise-missile and drone interceptors, xTech|Apex Intercept $8 million more, xTech|Adaptive Strike $1.5 million for affordable drones, loitering munitions, and counter-UAS, and the open-topic xTech|Search 10 drew more than 1,900 submissions. New events are coming at CES in January and in a dismounted-interceptor competition after that. As the Army’s chief technology officer, Dr. Alex Miller, puts it, xTech is the Army’s way of “surveying the industry landscape and fast-tracking promising technology rather than falling victim to years-long studies” (Army FUZE xTech, Q4 FY26 snapshot). One caution worth keeping in view, voiced in industry commentary this month: as mission software increasingly gets written — and thrown away — at the edge by AI-assisted coders, “we won’t need to treat software as a permanent asset,” which is a bracing idea for vendors whose business model assumes the opposite. The reward goes to firms that show up open, fast, and willing to be measured by Soldiers.

I/ITSEC Comes to Orlando — With the Army in the Chair

The autonomy story is also a training story, and next week the training world gathers only a six hour drive from Columbus. I/ITSEC — the Interservice/Industry Training, Simulation and Education Conference, the largest modeling-and-simulation and training event in the world, run by the National Training and Simulation Association (NTSA) — convenes at the Orange County Convention Center in Orlando from November 30 to December 3. The 2026 theme is “Beyond the Battlefield: Training Readiness and Lethality for All-Domain Dominance,” and, notably for this issue, the U.S. Army is the host service — a signal of how central synthetic training, simulation, and now autonomy-in-the-loop have become to landpower. Last year’s gathering drew close to 16,000 participants and more than 500 exhibitors from 55 nations, so expect the floor to be the clearest single read on where training technology is heading (NTSA).

For this region, I/ITSEC is close to home turf. Modeling and simulation is the Chattahoochee Valley’s and Fort Benning’s deep bench, and the show is where the forthcoming Pentagon modeling-and-simulation strategy — the first in thirty years, which we covered two issues ago — meets the vendors who will have to execute it. Watch three threads in particular: how AI is being validated for use inside simulations rather than merely bolted on; how live, virtual, and constructive training is stitching autonomous systems into the force; and the workforce question, where NTSA’s Certified Modeling & Simulation Professional credential runs its prep workshops on site. If you build, buy, or staff training technology, this is the week to be paying attention.

A recurring feature spotlighting our own reading of the ideas, people, and places shaping our region.

Smarter, Not Smaller

In a research paper published this week, the Royal United Services Institute’s Dr. Jack Watling — whose book The Arms of the Future we reviewed on this site — and his colleague Robert Tollast turn from theory to the infantry battalion, and arrive at a conclusion worth pinning to the wall. Tactical drones, they find, are now essential: they improve a battalion’s ability to see, strike, survive, and maneuver. But they are “not a substitute for infantry, mortars, anti-tank weapons or skilled reconnaissance personnel,” and — against the hope that autonomy will thin the ranks — they conclude the opposite. Integrating uncrewed systems reshapes a battalion’s structure, training, and logistics; it does not shrink its manpower. Drones are personnel-intensive. Someone must fly, fix, protect, and resupply them, and someone must kill the enemy’s autonomous systems.

The recommendations are refreshingly concrete: plan for the operators and maintainers that drones actually require; continue to execute combined arms rather than trading mortars and anti-tank weapons for quadcopters; shift reconnaissance toward uncrewed surveillance while preserving sniper-grade fieldcraft; build dedicated counter-drone interception teams into every plan; make last-mile logistics cheap and attritable, designed for frequent losses; and protect infantry mass, because taking and holding ground still decides things. Read against this issue’s enthusiasms — the swarm at Grafenwoehr, the 128-hour contract, the prize money — the Watling-Tollast paper is the ballast. It is not a brake on modernization; it is a reminder of what modernization costs, and of the truth running through Mingus’s essay too: the machines are changing how the Army fights, but they are not changing who is responsible for the fight. Smarter, yes. Smaller, no.

By Audience

What This Week Means for You

01 For Business Owners & Small Business

  • The door is open and fast: the FUZE / Jailbreak / NANO ACE pathway can turn a commercial system into an Army agreement in days — but only if you’ve opened your interfaces and published standard APIs to the Army API Marketplace. Integration-readiness is now the price of entry.
  • Where the prize money is: Army xTech has open competitions with cash on the table — Disrupt Fires ($20M), Apex Intercept ($8M), Adaptive Strike ($1.5M), and the open-topic Search 10 — plus xTech|Live at CES in January. Start at xtech.army.mil.
  • Build to be measured: Soldiers hold the majority vote in these events. Design to be demonstrated, scored, and connected — not briefed.

02 For Investors

  • Value is shifting from platforms to orchestration: autonomy is being fielded as formations, not single systems, so data integration, open interfaces, and counter-UAS may matter more than any one airframe.
  • Mind the manpower math: the RUSI finding is that drones add operators, maintainers, logistics, and counter-drone teams. This is a services, sustainment, and training market as much as a hardware one — not a headcount-reduction play.
  • The acquisition machine is compressing time-to-contract (128-hour awards; two dozen xTech competitions coming) — faster revenue for firms that arrive API-ready, but shorter software lifecycles for those who don’t.

03 For Government & Community Leaders

  • Set expectations honestly: the autonomy curve has real costs — one robotic mission in five still ends in an emergency stop, and the force gets more complex, not leaner. Adoption is a training and sustainment problem, not just a buying one.
  • I/ITSEC (Orlando, Nov. 30–Dec. 3), with the Army as host service, puts the region’s modeling-and-simulation strength in the national spotlight — and the CMSP-credentialed workforce is a local asset worth cultivating.
  • The Future Ground Maneuver Summit comes to the Columbus Convention Center March 30–31, 2027, with WSG’s Chris Willis facilitating an innovation panel.
