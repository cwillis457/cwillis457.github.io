Book Review

Cassino ’44

The Brutal Battle for Rome — by James Holland

Reviewed by COL (Ret) Chris R. Willis

AuthorJames Holland
Published2024 · Atlantic Monthly Press
Length672 pages
CategoryMilitary History · Italian Campaign
Best ForStudents of sustained large-scale combat operations
SeriesItalian Campaign Trilogy, Book 3

The Italian Campaign of 1943 and 1944 was the logical next step in the Allies’ plan to attack the ‘soft underbelly’ of the European Axis Powers — an economy of force campaign to knock Italy out of the war, fix German divisions away from France and the Eastern Front, and buy time for Operation Overlord. In Cassino ’44: The Brutal Battle for Rome, the concluding volume of his Italian Campaign trilogy, James Holland picks up where The Savage Storm left off: January 1944, with the Allies stalled against the Gustav Line in the worst Italian winter in memory. The five months that followed produced two of the war’s most infamous battles — the four ferocious assaults on Monte Cassino and the fraught landing in the marshes at Anzio — before Rome finally fell on 4 June 1944, just two days before D-Day.

Holland is an English historian, prolific author, broadcaster, and podcaster (We Have Ways of Making You Talk). Cassino ’44 completes the trilogy begun with Sicily ’43: The First Assault on Fortress Europe (2021) and continued in The Savage Storm: The Battle for Italy 1943 (2023), both reviewed on this site. At a substantial 672 pages and built on twenty years of research, this volume provides the modern military leader with hard lessons about sustained large-scale combat operations against a determined defender in restrictive terrain, the costs of frontal assault when operational alternatives fail, and the burden of command when every option is bad.

Holland’s most valuable contribution is a revisionist one. The standard narrative treats the drive on Rome as a bungled campaign of needless frontal attacks, with Lieutenant General Mark Clark cast as its vainglorious villain. Holland, drawing on the full documentary record, argues that given the brutal conditions and the constant shortage of men and materiel — deliberately held back for the D-Day build-up — Clark and his fellow commanders led a remarkably successful campaign. Readers of The Savage Storm review on this site will recall Clark’s underestimation of German resistance at Salerno; Holland’s reassessment here does not excuse those earlier errors, but it does restore proportion. Commanders must be judged against the resources they were given and the strategic priorities they served, not against the unconstrained campaigns historians can fight from an armchair. That is a standard worth remembering in any after action review.

The human cost anchors the book. Well more than 100,000 Allied casualties fell in the five months leading to Rome — more than in any other campaign of the war — and senior commanders on both sides compared the fighting to Passchendaele and the Somme. Holland’s signature method, weaving diaries, memoirs, and interviews from American, British, Polish, German, New Zealand, Indian, and French colonial soldiers alongside Italian civilians caught in the maelstrom, gives the reader a true feel for combat across echelons — from riflemen scaling Monastery Hill to the army group deliberations over whether to bomb the ancient Benedictine abbey. His treatment of that decision — the destruction of Monte Cassino’s monastery in February 1944 — is a case study in targeting ethics, military necessity, and the information effects of destruction that resonates directly with today’s debates over strikes on culturally protected sites.

For the student of modern large-scale combat operations, the campaign offers uncomfortable lessons. Anzio demonstrates that an amphibious turning movement without the combat power and command aggressiveness to exploit it merely creates a second stalemate — conditions setting, again, proving decisive. The four Cassino assaults show what happens when air power, artillery mass, and raw courage are asked to substitute for maneuver in mountains that negate mechanized advantages — terrain that today’s planners contemplating operations in the Pacific’s mountainous littorals or the Caucasus would do well to study. And the multinational character of the final breakthrough — Polish, French colonial, British, and American formations attacking in concert — previews the coalition integration challenges that dominate Allied operations to this day.

In summary, James Holland’s Cassino ’44 is a worthy and definitive conclusion to his look at the Allies’ campaign against the ‘soft underbelly’ of the Axis Powers. Holland’s expertise in WWII history, detailed first person narratives, and engaging narrative style make for an excellent book that memorializes a true turning point in the European Theater of Operations (ETO) of WWII — and completes, alongside Sicily ’43 and The Savage Storm, the finest modern account of the Italian Campaign available. This book provides pertinent lessons for the modern student of large-scale ground combat operations (LSCO).
