Book Review

Victory ’45

The End of the War in Eight Surrenders — by James Holland with Al Murray

Reviewed by COL (Ret) Chris R. Willis

AuthorJames Holland with Al Murray
Published2025 · Atlantic Monthly Press
Length400 pages
CategoryMilitary History · War Termination
Best ForLeaders who plan endings, not just operations
RelatedThe War in the West, Vols. 1–2

Wars are easier to start than to end. Published for the 80th anniversary of VE Day, Victory ’45: The End of the War in Eight Surrenders takes the part of the Second World War we think we know best — the ending — and shows how contingent, chaotic, and human it actually was. James Holland, joined here by his We Have Ways of Making You Talk podcast partner Al Murray, structures the book as eight chapters around eight surrender ceremonies, from the Italian Alps and Lüneburg Heath to Reims, Berlin, and finally the deck of the USS Missouri. Hitler himself never surrendered; what followed his suicide was not a clean capitulation but a scramble of negotiations, rivalries, and last-minute maneuvering that shaped the postwar world.

For readers of this site, Victory ’45 occupies a particular place in the Holland shelf. His War in the West series — The Rise of Germany and The Allies Strike Back, both reviewed here — was planned as a trilogy whose concluding 1943–45 volume has never appeared. This book is not that volume: it is a different kind of work, co-authored, episodic, and focused tightly on the war’s final weeks rather than its final campaigns. But it is the closest thing to an ending Holland has yet written for the arc, and taken on its own terms, it delivers something the operational histories rarely examine: the mechanics of conflict termination.

Those mechanics reward professional study. The eight surrenders were not a formality but a series of negotiations conducted under enormous friction: SS leaders like Himmler attempting to bargain their way past accountability through back channels to Allen Dulles in Switzerland; German commanders racing to surrender westward rather than to the Soviets; Allied officers accepting capitulations from forces that had beaten them in earlier battles; and in the Pacific, an emperor navigating fanatical resistance within his own government even after two atomic bombs. Unconditional surrender was a policy set at Casablanca in 1943 — but converting that policy into eight signed instruments required improvisation, judgment, and restraint at every level. War termination, the book demonstrates, is an operation in itself, and one for which almost nobody had planned.

The lessons carry forward directly. Modern doctrine talks about “consolidating gains” and end states, yet recent history — from Iraq to Afghanistan — shows how badly endings go when the surrender-to-stability transition is an afterthought. Victory ’45 is a case-study anthology in exactly that transition: who has authority to surrender, how terms are communicated to forces still fighting, what happens to leaders attempting to escape accountability, and how victors’ conduct in the first days sets the trajectory of the peace. Leaders who plan operations without planning terminations should read this book as a warning.

Honest assessment requires noting what the book is not. The episodic, character-driven style — well suited to newcomers and clearly shaped by the authors’ podcast chemistry — trades away the analytical depth of Holland’s solo work; readers who came for the logistics ledgers of the War in the West volumes will find a lighter touch here, and some longtime readers have missed it. The co-authored voice occasionally smooths over the sharp operational analysis that distinguishes Holland’s campaign histories. These are choices, not flaws, but they place the book closer to accessible popular history than to the professional bookshelf’s analytical core.

In summary, Victory ’45 is a gripping, humane account of how the largest war in history actually stopped — messily, unevenly, and through the judgment of individuals under pressure. Read the War in the West volumes for how the Allies built the power to win; read this for the reminder that winning is not an event but a negotiated, managed, and fragile process. For the modern leader, the book’s enduring question is uncomfortable and essential: you have planned the fight — have you planned the finish?
