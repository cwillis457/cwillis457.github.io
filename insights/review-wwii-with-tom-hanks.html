Series Review · Documentary / Podcast

World War II with Tom Hanks

The HISTORY Channel · Back Pocket Studios · Audacy — narrated by Tom Hanks

Reviewed by COL (Ret) Chris R. Willis

NarratorTom Hanks
Executive ProducersTom Hanks · Jon Meacham
ProducersHISTORY Channel · Back Pocket Studios · Audacy
ReleasedMemorial Day 2026 · 20 hours
PartnerNational WWII Museum, New Orleans
CategoryDocumentary / Podcast · WWII

This site usually reviews books, but the same standard applies to any serious work about the profession of arms: does it inform how we lead, think, and remember? World War II with Tom Hanks — a twenty-hour series released on Memorial Day 2026, available both as a HISTORY Channel docuseries and as a podcast through Back Pocket Studios and Audacy — clears that bar. Executive produced by Hanks and Pulitzer-winning historian Jon Meacham, and developed in collaboration with the National WWII Museum in New Orleans, it is the most ambitious single-narrator survey of the war since Laurence Olivier’s The World at War in the 1970s, a comparison the producers invite and, for the most part, earn.

Hanks is the obvious and the right choice to guide it. His screen work — Saving Private Ryan, Greyhound, and the producing trilogy of Band of Brothers, The Pacific, and Masters of the Air — has made him, as one profile put it, America’s chief chronicler of the conflict. What matters for the listener is that he narrates all twenty episodes not as read copy but as a participant in the storytelling, and it shows: the narration humanizes rather than lectures. Across the full arc — the rise of fascism, Poland 1939, Pearl Harbor, the fall of Berlin, Hiroshima, and the uneasy peace after — each hour reexamines a facet of the war through archival footage, expert commentary, and the human stories that Hanks has spent a career insisting we not forget.

For the leader and the professional, the series’ value is twofold. First, as accessible command education: its global, every-theater sweep is an efficient way for a young officer, an NCO, or a family member to acquire the shape of the whole war before diving into the campaign-level depth of the books reviewed on this site — Holland’s Italian Campaign trilogy, Pacific Blitzkrieg, or the operational-level War in the West volumes. Think of it as the map before the terrain walk. Second, and more subtly, it is itself a case study in strategic communication: how a society transmits the hard lessons of its costliest war to a generation with no living memory of it, and why that transmission is a leadership responsibility, not merely an entertainment product.

That transmission theme is the series’ deliberate throughline. Hanks and Meacham are explicit that they made it for this moment — the 80th anniversary, the passing of the last veterans, and what they see as a time when the war’s lessons are at risk of being forgotten. The recurring question the series presses — how does this reflect our behavior today? — is exactly the reflective habit that good leader development tries to instill: history not as nostalgia, but as a mirror. When Hanks describes the war as asking an entire generation to put the present on hold for a moral task, he is articulating something every leader who has asked sacrifice of others will recognize.

An honest assessment notes the form’s limits. A twenty-hour survey of a global war is necessarily broad, not deep; specialists and serious students will find familiar ground covered at altitude, and the emotional, human-centered approach that is the series’ great strength occasionally comes at the expense of operational and strategic granularity. The choice to foreground individual stories and moral reflection over doctrine and decision-making is the right one for its mass audience, but it means the series complements the professional reading list rather than substituting for it. It is the on-ramp, not the destination.

In summary, World War II with Tom Hanks is a landmark, humane, and genuinely useful retelling of the war for a generation that needs it — superb narration, serious historical partnership, and a clear-eyed sense of why the telling still matters. For the professional, it is the ideal companion to the deeper campaign histories on this site: start here for the arc and the human stakes, then turn to the books for the operational lessons. And for anyone who leads: watch or listen with the series’ own question in mind — not just what happened, but what it asks of us now.
