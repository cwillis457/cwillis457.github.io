Issue 09 — The Drone Force

A weekly briefing for owners, investors, government leaders, and small businesses in the Columbus–Fort Benning region.

Issue 09  ·  Week of November 16, 2026

The Army is rebuilding itself around cheap, expendable autonomy, and this issue follows that thread from the strategy down to the squad. A new think-tank study warns that land power faces questions it has not had to answer since the Cold War. The force’s reply is already in the field: drone warfare taught at every level, drones bought by the tens of thousands and flown until they break, standard mortars clipped onto UAS, and the AI and lasers racing to find and kill the drones on the other side. We end closer to home — with the capital and workforce lining up behind it, and, from the WSG desk, a Columbus story worth your weekend.

This Week’s Lead

A New Study Puts Hard Questions to American Land Power

Facing what it calls “major modernization questions,” the U.S. Army is being pressed to fold a decade of technological change into its very idea of land power — and a new report argues the stakes are higher than at any point since the Cold War. Produced by the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) with support from the Association of the U.S. Army, as part of a yearlong study on the future of land warfare, “Preparing U.S. Land Forces to Fight in an Era of Strategic and Technological Disruption” holds that advances in information warfare, command and control, and intelligence have “democratized lethality and challenged conventional assumptions about how to prevail on the battlefield.” U.S. land power, it warns, now “faces an even more complex operational environment than the Cold War,” one that requires the military and its combatant commanders to “reassess their global posture and regional theater architecture.”

The report credits the Army’s own transformation — the Army Transformation Initiative and the new “How the Army Fights” guidance, which implements the service’s strategic vision through four elements: convergence, strike, close battle, and endurance. With brigade roles redefined and capabilities pulled up to the division level, it says, the Army must now wrestle with first-order design questions it has deferred for years: “How big should divisions be?” and what is the right span of control for today’s battlefield geometry. Going forward, the authors write, the United States “must make important decisions over where to prioritize global posture, how to train and retain talent, and which capabilities present the best investment” — framing the problem as both a posture challenge, over how far forward to physically position forces to deter, and a tactical one, over giving each echelon a clear operational focus.

The sharpest finding is a warning. “It is not clear,” the report states, “that U.S. Army training institutions are integrating lessons from Ukraine into doctrine and operations sufficiently, quickly, or deeply,” and it notes that U.S.–Ukraine training collaboration has “diminished due to competing priorities.” That is a consequential charge, because the rest of this issue is, in effect, the Army’s counter-argument — the service moving at speed to teach, buy, and field exactly the drone capabilities the report worries it is absorbing too slowly. The report is independent analysis, not Army policy. (CSIS, with AUSA).

The Force Answers

Drone Warfare Comes to Every Echelon

If the study asks whether the Army is learning fast enough, the training enterprise is the Army’s reply. Drone warfare “is being taught and operationalized at every level” of the force, two senior Army technology officers said recently, and today’s Soldiers already show a striking aptitude for it. The demand signal is concrete: in December 2025 the Pentagon stood up a $1 billion, two-year program to buy American-made small, lethal drones and build combat power quickly across the services. Inside the Army, Soldiers in operational and experimental units are learning to fly a range of systems, and a new military occupational specialty — the MOS 15X tactical UAS specialist — now trains in advanced individual training at Fort Huachuca, Arizona (AUSA).

What the officers describe is a generational shift, not a procurement line. Alex Miller, the Army’s chief technology officer, said an early fear — that the service would “stovepipe” drones and treat a “$1,000 quadcopter the same way that we do an Apache gunship” — simply is not materializing: most operators “are not aviators, they’re infantrymen and women, they are logistics folks,” and the trainers understand the technology well enough not to bias toward treating every drone like a manned aircraft. Col. Greg Merkl, who directs the Army’s drone-dominance program, put it generationally — his eleven-year-old already uses a VR headset and could learn to fly a drone with ease: “It’s going to be second nature to him.” Increasingly, Merkl said, Soldiers arrive already holding the skill, the way they once arrived knowing how to use a computer; the institution’s job now is to gather feedback “from the actual force down at the tactical level.”

The Economics of Expendability

Fly Them Until They Break

Teaching a force to fly drones only matters if there are drones to fly, and the Army has decided there will be a great many of them, briefly. Its cheapest small attack drones will not be stored or repaired; they will be built, delivered, flown “until they break,” and replaced. A July 2025 directive from the defense secretary ordered that Group 1 and Group 2 drones — those up to 55 pounds — be accounted for as consumables rather than durable property, and the Army is testing the approach under a framework called Rapid Attritable Commercial-Off-the-Shelf Strike. Jon Strzelec, the advanced-manufacturing lead for small drones at Army Materiel Command, says the math is unforgiving: a new drone design can be countered within roughly 72 hours, and the fast part of the innovation cycle runs about three months, so the word for these systems is “replace,” not “sustain.” One contract-management officer has argued the Pentagon should be willing to lose perhaps 100,000 small drones a year, simply to learn from them (Breaking Defense).

The numbers behind the “Drone Dominance” competition — the vehicle for that $1 billion, two-year effort — show the scale: first base orders for 22,320 drones went out in March to eleven winning vendors, and the Army was receiving the first 10,000 of an initial 30,000 as of the interview. A larger successor to the Replicator initiative, the Defense Autonomous Warfare Group, is planned at nearly $55 billion. Not everyone is sanguine. Analysts at the Atlantic Council and the Council on Foreign Relations warn there is no clear long-term sustainment plan and no stated approach to obsolete components and software; a fleet with no reserve leans entirely on its supply chain and on surging production in wartime, and the Army has not publicly explained how it would weather a disruption. A West Point Modern War Institute essay splits the difference, arguing for “repair forward, replace fast” — bounded repair decisions made by technicians near the front. The honest bottleneck, more than one official conceded, may not be hardware at all but the supply of trained pilots.

Cheap Lethality, Clipped On

A Kit That Turns Standard Mortars Into Drone Munitions

If the drones are expendable, so is what they drop — and the Army would rather not buy a brand-new munition to do it. At the DEVCOM Armaments Center at Picatinny Arsenal, engineers have built the Munitions Air-dropped Lethal Effects Tool, or MALET: a clip-on conversion kit that lets standard 60mm and 81mm mortar rounds be dropped from a drone instead. The kit adds the fuze function and the aerodynamic stability a drop requires, and it is non-destructive and reversible. Nothing is permanently modified and no safety mechanism is manually removed; if the mission changes, the kit comes off and the round is fired as a conventional mortar. It draws on existing inventory rather than a new production line, and the architecture could extend beyond the 60mm and 81mm rounds the team started with (Army.mil).

The work began in 2024, and in a recent test live 60mm rounds dropped from a moving drone oriented correctly and detonated in the target area. Ryan Gorman, a senior scientific and technical manager for indirect-fire munitions, called it “a unique and viable option for the warfighter,” with further concepts still close-hold; Brad Armstrong leads the project. The team plans to put the kit in front of Soldiers at major fall exercises, then validate it and transition it to industry partners and the acquisition community for rapid scaling. There are no published quantities, costs, or timelines yet — treat MALET as promising R&D rather than a fielded capability — but the logic is the same one running through this issue: make lethality cheap, modular, and fast to field.

The Eyes and the Shield

Finding the Targets, and Killing the Drones

Two technologies bracket the drone fight: the AI that helps find targets, and the systems that knock enemy drones down. On the first, the DEVCOM C5ISR Center at Fort Belvoir is hardening Aided Target Recognition — the machine-learning models that automatically detect and flag targets in drone imagery — for the degraded conditions Soldiers face. Commercial models “often assume optimal conditions,” the center notes, while real targets sit distant, camouflaged, and under terrain cover; the Army’s aim is to detect them faster, cut the Soldier’s cognitive load, and shorten the kill chain. The training method is intuitive: the Army curated imagery of tactically relevant targets across many environments, and, as electronics engineer Grace Hummel put it, the algorithm “learns to identify the unique visual patterns of these objects on its own, much like a Soldier studying flashcards before a vehicle recognition test.” The center is integrating the capability with the Aviation and Missile Center’s teaming and mission-autonomy software ahead of a live flight test at Yuma Proving Ground, and is working with industry to put Army-tailored recognition onto commercial drone platforms (U.S. Army / DEVCOM C5ISR Center).

On the other side of the fight, the counter-drone market is going layered. Allen Control Systems — maker of the Bullfrog, an AI-controlled, gun-based system that shoots small drones out of the sky, and fresh off counter-UAS contracts with South Korea and the UAE — is reported to be standing up a new directed-energy division and recruiting former U.S. Army laser leaders to run it, pairing kinetic defeat with high-energy lasers under one roof. The direction is unmistakable, and it tracks the Army’s own push to move high-energy lasers such as the Locust system toward fielding later this decade: the same transparency that makes massed drones possible is driving a parallel race to see them first and strike them cheapest — on both ends of the kill chain (The Defense Post; trade reporting).

Closer to Home

The Capital, the Place, and the Workforce

The hardware in this issue must be built, sited, and staffed somewhere, and Georgia spent the past year making the case that it should be here. The state posted a record economic-development year in fiscal 2026 — roughly $35.2 billion in investment across 436 projects and about 25,300 expected new private-sector jobs, Gov. Brian Kemp’s office announced — with more than $7.8 billion in expected foreign direct investment. Two numbers matter most for this region: 75 percent of those expansions and new locations landed outside metro Atlanta, and manufacturing accounted for 67 percent of the new jobs. Korea was the top international source of investment, a useful backdrop to the Korean-led manufacturing growth already reshaping the Chattahoochee Valley corridor. The release names no Columbus project specifically — it is state context, not a local win — but it describes exactly the environment the Dispatch’s readers are operating in (Georgia Dept. of Economic Development).

Locally, Choose Columbus is the front door. The city’s economic-development team lists defense, aerospace, and “manufacturing and robotics” among its target industries, runs a Military Talent Pipeline built on the region’s veteran workforce, and points industry to shovel-ready sites, rail, and intermodal access toward the Port of Savannah. Its project list mixes committed wins — a Pratt & Whitney presence, a J.M. Smucker expansion, a Columbus State University technology center — with at least one marquee announcement, a $223 million advanced-magnet facility, that local reporting now describes as paused; worth confirming before anyone banks on it. The throughline from the state’s record year to the local front door is labor, and here a new federal tool helps. Workforce Pell, effective in July 2026 and administered in Georgia by the Technical College System of Georgia, extends Pell grants to short-term training programs of roughly eight to fourteen weeks in fields like manufacturing, healthcare, and IT. To qualify, programs must hit real outcomes, better than seventy percent completion and job placement and credentials validated by local employers, which is to say it is built to produce workers the region’s plants can use (Choose Columbus; Technical College System of Georgia).

WSG Insights

From the Willis Strategy Group Desk

A recurring feature spotlighting our own reading of the ideas, people, and places shaping our region. This week, a change of register — a book.

Theo of Golden: A Columbus Story

Every so often a book belongs to a place as much as to its author, and Columbus now has one. Theo of Golden, the debut novel by Columbus native Allen Levi, began as a quietly self-published story in 2023 and became a genuine phenomenon — picked up by Atria Books, climbing to number one among all titles on Amazon in early 2026, and selling well past 750,000 copies. Its premise is deceptively gentle: an elderly stranger named Theo arrives in a river town called Golden and undertakes a quiet campaign of attention, commissioning portraits of ordinary townspeople and giving them away. Golden is unmistakably Columbus — its coffee shop has a real-world cousin in Fountain City Coffee, and Visit Columbus has built a self-guided tour of the places behind the story.

The Dispatch usually trains its eye on the hard architecture of this region — the installations, the industry, the drones. Theo of Golden is a reminder of what all of it is finally for. A town worth investing in is first a town worth seeing, and Levi has given Columbus a portrait of itself as a place where ordinary kindness is still possible and still consequential — civic capital, not soft sentiment. We recommend it warmly. Read our full note, “Theo of Golden: A Columbus Story,” on the WSG Insights page.

What This Week Means for You

01 For Business Owners & Small Business

  • Drones are a volume business now: the $1 billion “Drone Dominance” buy plus “replace, not repair” means steady, high-volume demand for cheap American drones, components, and conversion kits — eleven vendors are already on contract, and the Army’s in-house effort has pivoted to components.
  • The Army wants commercial partners: C5ISR is integrating target-recognition AI onto commercial drone platforms, and MALET is headed for an industry transition — concrete openings for payload, software, and manufacturing firms.
  • Staff the surge with no-debt training: Workforce Pell GA (via TCSG) funds short 8–14-week manufacturing and IT credentials; pair it with the LYNX supplier on-ramp (lynxconnect.io) to get into the defense supply chain.

02 For Investors

  • Consumable drones are a recurring-revenue story — but mind the risk: a no-reserve fleet depends on supply-chain resilience and wartime surge, and trained pilots, not hardware, may be the real limiter. Watch the eleven Drone Dominance vendors, Neros, and Performance Drone Works.
  • Counter-UAS is going layered — kinetic plus directed energy (Allen Control Systems) — alongside the Army’s own high-energy laser push toward late-decade fielding.
  • Georgia logged a record investment year, with manufacturing driving two-thirds of new jobs and Korea the top international source — the macro signal under the regional buildout.

03 For Government & Community Leaders

  • The CSIS study’s posture and force-design questions are the strategic backdrop; its warning that the Army may be absorbing drone and Ukraine lessons too slowly is the risk worth tracking — and the regional experimentation base is part of the answer.
  • The state’s record year, Choose Columbus’s front door, and Workforce Pell together align capital, place, and labor for the region — a story worth telling with one voice.
